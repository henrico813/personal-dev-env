# Grammar terms

The terms to use when naming the parts of a sentence. Most examples
come from this Go doc comment:

> Each old must be whole "//" lines found exactly once in its file,
> starting a line, with no comment line just above or below; a Go
> directive just above or below is allowed. Reports a problem for
> every bad item and rename together, rather than stopping at the
> first.

## Subject and predicate

The subject is who or what the sentence is about. The predicate is
what is said about the subject. In "Each old must be whole lines", the
subject is "Each old" and the predicate is "must be whole lines".

## Clause and phrase

A clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb, such as "a Go
directive is allowed". A phrase is a group of words that work together
without their own complete subject and verb, such as "in its file".

## Independent and dependent clauses

An independent clause can stand alone as a sentence. A dependent
clause usually cannot. "Because the file contains a duplicate ..."
leaves the reader waiting for the rest.

A semicolon joins two related independent clauses. In the example, it
separates the rule from its exception, "a Go directive just above or
below is allowed".

## Declarative clauses

A declarative clause makes a statement. A question is interrogative,
and a command is imperative. All three clauses in the example are
declarative.

## Elliptical sentences

An elliptical sentence leaves out its subject because the context
supplies it. "Reports a problem for every bad item" means "The
function reports a problem for every bad item".

## Modifiers, adjectives and adverbs

A modifier is a word or phrase that adds detail, such as "exactly
once". An adjective describes or limits a noun, as "whole" does in
"whole lines" and "bad" in "bad item". The adverbs in the example are
"exactly", which modifies "once", "just", which narrows "above or
below" to the next line, and "together", which modifies the
reporting.

## Determiners

A determiner says which or how many, not what kind. "Each" treats
items one at a time, "every" covers all of them, and "no" excludes
them, as in "no comment line". Traditional school grammar groups
these with adjectives.

## Finite and nonfinite verbs

A finite verb anchors a clause, such as "Reports" or "is". A nonfinite
form depends on another part of the sentence. "found" is a past
participle, "starting" is a present participle, and "stopping" is a
gerund-participle.

A group built on a nonfinite form is a nonfinite clause, which school
grammar calls a participial or gerund phrase. "found exactly once in
its file", "starting a line" and "rather than stopping at the first"
add conditions but cannot stand alone.

## Linking verbs and complements

A linking verb such as "be" connects the subject to a property. The
property is the complement. In "Each old must be whole lines", the
complement is "whole lines".

## Active and passive voice

In active voice the actor is the subject, as in "The function allows
a Go directive". In passive voice the thing acted on becomes the
subject, as in "A Go directive is allowed", and the actor can drop out.

## Modals

The modal "must" expresses an obligation. "Each old is a whole line"
states a fact. "Each old must be a whole line" states a
requirement.

## Attributive nouns

A noun can modify another noun, as "coffee" does in "coffee cup". In
"comment line", "comment" says what kind of line, and in "Go
directive", "Go" says which directive.

## Prepositions and nearby adverbs

A preposition introduces a noun phrase and sets a relation of place,
direction, time, manner or purpose. In the example, "in its file"
gives a place, "with no comment line" a condition, "for every bad
item" the targets, "at the first" the stopping point, and "rather than
stopping" the rejected choice.

"above" and "below" appear with no object. Some grammars call them
adverbs there, and many modern grammars call them intransitive
prepositions.

## Conjunctions and coordination

A conjunction connects words, phrases or clauses, such as "and", "or"
and "rather than". In "every bad item and rename", "and" joins
"rename" to "item", so the reader cannot tell whether a rename is a
kind of bad item or a separate thing to report.

## Number

Number is singular or plural. "Each old must be whole lines" has a
singular subject and a plural complement, so the reader cannot tell
whether one old is one line or several.
